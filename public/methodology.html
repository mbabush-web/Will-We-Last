What moves the odds

Some answers matter a lot. Most matter a little.

Numbers are how much the yearly odds of a breakup change, compared with otherwise similar couples.

5.9×

A dating relationship that feels "fair or poor." How the relationship feels is the strongest sign we found, compared with couples who call theirs excellent.

1.6×

Two or more earlier live-in partners before moving in with this one.

1.6×

A spouse with children from another relationship. It was the one new signal among dozens of extra questions we tested.

0.6×

A bachelor's degree when moving in together, compared with a high school diploma.

0.76×

Marrying since 2010 compared with the 2000s, for couples of the same age and background. Divorce keeps falling.

1.0×

A wife who earns more than her husband. It made no difference once the husband's pay was known (PSID, 7,115 couples). A low-earning husband is the signal.

≈1×

Personality and test scores. The Big Five, self-esteem and aptitude scores barely changed the predictions.

≈1×

Income at the wedding. Adding earnings didn't improve the wedding-day model, except that no earnings at all raised the odds a little.

How far to trust it

Good at averages, modest at telling couples apart

The levels are right. When the model says 30% of couples like you split up within five years, about 30% of those couples did. We checked this on people the model never saw.

Ranking is modest. Given two couples, it picks the one that splits first about two times in three. Most of what ends a relationship isn't in a questionnaire, and that ceiling held in every survey we tried.

Dating is the roughest. It rests on about 680 couples, so its high and low ends are less certain than the married model's.

The full accuracy checks

Every model, tested on couples it never saw

For readers who want the details: how close each model's odds came to what actually happened, and how well it told couples apart.

Calibration. When a model says 20%, about 20% of those couples split up. Each dot is a tenth of couples sorted by risk (a fifth for dating), using predictions from 5-fold cross-validation (the model never saw those people) and Kaplan–Meier for what happened.

5 years10 years
  • Dating is the roughest estimate. About 680 couples who were dating when first asked, in 2009 or 2017, so the ranking (concordance 0.64) is decent but the high and low ends are shakier than for marriages. About 6 in 10 had split up within five years. How the relationship felt at the start mattered most: "fair or poor" went with about five times the yearly odds of "excellent".
  • Living together splits sooner, then settles. Of NLSY97 couples who moved in together, 22% split within a year and 53% within five; by five years, most of the couples still together had married each other. Earlier live-in partners raised the odds (1.6 times for two or more); a bachelor's degree lowered them. In the national NSFG survey, couples who moved in since 2010 split as often in the first year as 2000s couples but less often after that (0.79 times the yearly odds), so the model is lowered after the first year.
  • It ranks couples modestly. A concordance near 0.65 means the model gives the higher risk to the marriage that ended first about 65% of the time. Most of what ends a marriage isn't in these questions.
  • Marrying now is the default for weddings. Divorce keeps falling. In the national NSFG survey, 21.7% of women's first marriages from the 2010s ended within 10 years, against 30.7% for 2000s weddings and 40.0% for 1980s weddings. Comparing like with like, recent weddings have about three quarters the odds, so this setting lowers the NLSY97 model by that much for the share of its weddings that came before 2010.
  • Pick your generation. Marriages of people born around 1960 broke up less often, and remarriages in that generation were barely riskier than first marriages. The NLSY97 model overstated their odds by 10 to 20 points, so the 1960 setting uses a model fit on NLSY79.
  • The 1960 setting only knows the background questions. NLSY79 didn't ask about living together, religious services, earlier partners or the spouse in the same way, so those answers borrow the NLSY97 effects and are centered so they don't shift the average.
  • Earnings did almost nothing, so we don't ask. Adding earnings didn't improve the wedding-day model; only having no earnings at all raised the odds a little. A wife earning more than her husband looked riskier in NLSY97, but in 7,115 PSID couples it made no difference once his pay was known. A low-earning husband is the signal, not a high-earning wife.
  • Personality and test scores added nothing. The Big Five, self-esteem, locus of control and aptitude scores barely moved the concordance. The one new signal was a spouse with children from another relationship, which went with about 1.6 times the odds.
  • How the marriage feels says less than you'd think. Closeness, care, conflict and commitment, asked early in the marriage, raised the concordance only from 0.65 to 0.66, and conflict alone carried all of that, so it is the only one we ask. Not living with your spouse was the strongest current sign.
  • Every question has to earn its place. We refit each model leaving out one question at a time and dropped the ones that didn't help it rank couples: religious services, mother's schooling, children born before, earnings, age difference, and (depending on the stage) pregnancy, married before, met online and children at home. Accuracy stayed the same (table above), with about a third fewer questions. A question can still matter for the few people it applies to, such as having no earnings at all, so a dropped question means a small effect on average, not none.
  • "Divorce" includes legal separation in NLSY97, dated to when it began. NLSY79 dates the end of the marriage as reported.
